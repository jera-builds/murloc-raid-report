Raid Readiness.

v1 · week of 8/18–8/20 · data: Aug-22 log audit of both nights

Average readiness this week: 77/100 (Tue 78, Thu 75). None of this measures how well anyone plays — it's whether they showed up prepared to play well, and everything on this page is free performance: zero skill required. The headline is a split picture: consumable discipline is good — 357 Light's Potential and 204 Potion of Recklessness were drunk across the two nights — but the failures cluster in three specific spots: potion choice (drinking the wrong potion for your spec, not skipping it), bare enchant slots left behind after loot night, and augment runes, which only 8/24 raiders have carved at all.

How we measure (and why) — Raid Readiness

Readiness isn't about how well you play — it's about whether you showed up prepared to play well. Five checks, all things you control completely before the pull button is even pressed.

Combat potions

A combat potion is a one-time burst you drink mid-fight — not before the pull, during it, timed to line up with your other cooldowns. We call it "free performance" because unlike a well-timed cooldown rotation, chugging a potion takes zero skill and zero practice — you just have to remember to do it, and to grab the right one for your character. This tier the three DPS/tank potions you'll see most are Light's Potential (a flat boost to your main stat), Potion of Recklessness (a bigger secondary-stat boost with a small tradeoff, best on classes that can afford the downside), and Draught of Rampant Abandon (the tank's pick — it drops a damage-absorbing void zone instead of just buffing you). Healers usually reach for Lightfused Mana Potion instead, since their bottleneck is resource, not damage. The wrong potion for your spec still helps a little — it's just leaving free performance on the table for no reason.

Flask

A flask is a one-hour buff you drink before the pull, like putting your shoes on before you leave the house — you don't lace up mid-jog, and you don't flask mid-fight. Top guilds treat it the same way: it's not a decision, it's a habit, because it's a stat gain that costs gold and zero attention once it's up. We check flask uptime as a percentage of fight time (not just "did you drink one") because a flask that falls off mid-raid — it happens, a late reapply, a long break between pulls — quietly costs you the rest of that pull just like forgetting it in the first place.

Food buff (Well Fed)

Well Fed is the buff you get from eating a feast or a personal food item before the pull — smaller than a flask, but still free, and it costs nothing but remembering to eat before the pull starts like everyone else at the table. We check it because it's the cheapest, easiest box on this whole list to tick, so a raider missing it isn't a gear problem or a skill problem — it's just an oversight, and the fastest one to fix.

Augment rune (Void-Touched)

Everything else on this readiness check is a consumable — you use it up and have to remember it again next raid. An augment rune is the opposite: you carve it into a piece of gear once, and it's permanent, giving a passive stat boost every fight forever after with no upkeep. Void-Touched is this tier's current rune; the old Ethereal rune is still permanent, just weaker, and worth upgrading. Because it's a one-time purchase and a one-time application, having no rune at all — or still running the old Ethereal one — isn't a "forgot tonight" problem, it's a to-do-list item that's been sitting untouched.

Enchants & gems

Enchants and gems are permanent-ish upgrades applied to a specific piece of gear — a stat boost baked into that item, which means the moment you replace the item, the enchant and gems go with it and the new piece starts bare. That's exactly why loot night is the most common time to see empty slots: you win an upgrade, wear it immediately because it's still a bigger number on paper, and don't re-enchant or re-socket it before the next pull. It's not hypothetical — most raiders who played both nights this week changed gear between them, and roughly a third of those actually went backwards on total enchants and gems after picking up new loot. We check it because a bare slot is a free stat sitting on the table that costs gold, not skill, to fix.

Talents & spec vs. the Archon build

We compare your talent build to Archon's top-parsing build for your spec on that boss — Archon aggregates what the best-performing players on Warcraft Logs are actually running, not just what a guide says on paper. A build that doesn't match the top build isn't an automatic fail — it's a flag: "this is off the beaten path, come explain why." Sometimes there's a good reason: a class discord has found something Archon's aggregate hasn't caught up to yet, or a specific fight calls for a swap Archon's per-boss data doesn't isolate. The flag just makes sure that reason exists, and isn't "I forgot to check."

Readiness vs. performance: these are graded separately on purpose. Performance (your parse, your median) measures how well you play once the pull starts — that takes skill, practice, and good execution under pressure. Readiness measures whether you showed up with your flask, food, rune, gems and enchants in place — every one of those checks is 100% effort and 0% skill, a list anyone can clear with five minutes before raid. Mixing the two into one score would let a naturally skilled player's parse paper over sloppy prep, or let a diligent-but-still-learning player's prep paper over a rough parse. Keeping them apart means each number tells you something true, and something different.
